Survey-RAG: Language-Queryable Aerial Memory

A drone surveys an area once, turning what it sees into a searchable spatial memory. Afterwards, it can answer natural-language requests like “go to the park with the fountain” by retrieving the matching view and flying to its recorded coordinates.

Full video Short

Motivation

After a disaster, responders often lack a complete picture of what areas are damaged or require assistance. A single drone survey can build a searchable spatial memory that can later be queried to locate damaged infrastructure, blocked routes, stranded people, or areas requiring urgent assessment.

How it works

Battery-tiered compute

Each model is placed where its energy cost is affordable. Image embedding runs in flight; retrieval is a cosine search taking milliseconds; a local VLM is invoked only when the margin between the top two candidates is thin; captioning happens offline while charging.

This is not a stylistic choice. Measured on the drone's actual companion computer, a SigLIP2 image embedding costs 0.14 J and a VLM verification costs 4.37 J, roughly thirty times more. A VLM cannot sit in the retrieval path of a battery-limited aircraft. A configuration with no VLM at all costs only −0.07 P@1.

Tiled dual-render embeddings instead of a detector

Each snap is indexed as 5 tiles (full frame plus four quadrants) under 2 renderings (raw and deglared), max-pooled at query time. A fountain is tiny inside a 60×45 m footprint and a single global embedding dilutes it away; quadrant embeddings recover most of what an open-vocabulary detector would add, with zero extra models, and the winning quadrant hands the flight controller a quarter-footprint offset toward the object.

The dual rendering came from a measured failure. Raw CLIP read sunlit water as pale grass, so every water query failed. Deglaring fixed all water queries but pushed a picnic-table snap from rank 24 to rank 54. Preprocessing shifted the operating point rather than lifting it, so both renderings are indexed and each query picks its own.

Geometric non-overlap

Snap centres are planned one camera footprint apart and flown serpentine with yaw locked to zero, so image-up is North in every frame and no two snaps overlap. PX4 treats every position setpoint as "stop here", so waypoints are placed only at lane ends and captures are triggered by position, so cruise speed is held down the whole lane, braking only at the turns.

What I learned

My first evaluation was circular, and it inverted the answer

I initially graded retrieval using pseudo-ground-truth derived from the system's own captions. Under that scheme a BM25 keyword baseline "won" at 0.85, because it was searching the answer key. Hand-labelling all 192 images across 14 categories inverted the ranking: BM25 was in fact the worst method at 0.64. Ground-truth hygiene decided which conclusion was real, and I would not have caught it without doing the labelling myself. The benchmark now carries three independent grading systems (human labels, official segmentation masks, and a VLM judge from a different model family than the retriever), which agree on 15 of 17 overlapping verdicts.

A small pilot pointed the wrong way

A 7-query pilot suggested CLIP and SigLIP2 were roughly equivalent. Scaling to 44 queries with honest labels showed the gap widening: 0.68 versus 0.86 overall, and 0.25 versus 0.75 on compositional queries. Backend choice outweighed every preprocessing trick I tested.

The limitation is scoped, not vague

Objects below roughly 1% of frame area defeat every configuration tested. A picnic table at 30 m ranks 24th under raw CLIP, 54th deglared, 46th dual-render, 36th under SigLIP2, and the captioner never names it, so no keyword bonus can rescue it. The fix is known: fly lower, tile finer, or add a detection pass.
