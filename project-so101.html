SO-101: Learning Manipulation from Demonstration

A full learning-from-demonstration stack on a physical SO-101 arm — teleoperated collection, synchronised dual-camera recording, and vision-language-action policies trained for pick-and-place. Three rounds of data collection, each one a deliberate change to where demonstrations were taken from, turned out to matter more than how many there were. All datasets and policies are public on the Hugging Face Hub.

Three rounds of data collection

The task never changed: pick up a green block and place it in a box. What changed across three rounds was how the starting position of the block was chosen when recording demonstrations. Each round was a response to the evaluation results of the one before it.

All recorded on a physical SO-101 follower arm at 30 fps in LeRobot v3.0 format, Apache-2.0. Every frame carries a top camera (640×480), a wrist camera, 6-DoF joint state and 6-DoF action.

Round 1 — no measured position

The first datasets were recorded without tracking where the block started. An ACT policy trained on them evaluated poorly. With no record of the starting positions there was also no way to ask the useful diagnostic question — whether the failures clustered anywhere in particular — because the coverage of the workspace was simply unknown.

Round 2 — measured, and spread across six cells

So the workspace was divided into a grid of six cells and demonstrations were drawn from every cell, with the starting cell recorded. This is the intuitive fix: cover the space evenly and the policy should learn the whole space. It produced the largest dataset of the three — 101 episodes, 55,001 frames — and the policy trained on it was still not good.

Round 3 — a single cell

As a diagnostic rather than a solution, the next dataset was recorded entirely from one cell. It is smaller than round 2 in both episodes and frames. The ACT policy trained on it performed markedly better than either predecessor.

The same demonstration budget, spread thin, taught less than a fraction of it concentrated

Round 2 had more episodes and nearly twice the frames of round 3, distributed across the whole workspace. Round 3 had fewer, all in one place. Round 3 won. Spreading a fixed number of demonstrations over six cells left every cell too thinly covered to learn from; concentrating them made one region dense enough to actually fit.

The honest reading is a limit, not a win. The round-3 policy is strong where it was demonstrated and degrades as the block moves away from that region — so its result is not evidence of general pick-and-place capability, and it should not be compared directly against the round-2 policy, which was asked to cover six times the area. What the comparison does establish is that demonstration density per unit of workspace, not total episode count, was the binding constraint. Covering the full workspace at round-3 density is the obvious next experiment, and it is a much larger collection effort than any round so far.

Evaluation

Evaluation rollouts are recorded as their own dataset, so a policy's behaviour is inspectable after the fact rather than reduced to a pass/fail tally. eval_pick_place_1_grid1 holds 9 rollouts of the round-3 ACT policy, 4,533 frames in total. The block was repositioned by hand between attempts rather than reset to a fixed pose, so the policy had to handle a starting position it had not been placed in.

Running the same ACT recipe across every round is what makes the rounds comparable — the architecture is held fixed and only the data changes. SmolVLA was trained on the round-2 data to check whether a pretrained vision-language backbone could recover what ACT could not from the same thinly-spread demonstrations. I also fine-tuned π0.5 on the recorded task, and integrated DiT-Flow — a diffusion transformer with a flow-matching objective — as a LeRobot policy package. DiT-Flow is an integration of existing work, not my own architecture.

Engineering

Cross-environment teleoperation

The LeRobot teleoperator stack and Isaac Sim require incompatible Python environments and cannot share a process. The leader arm is therefore read in its own interpreter and streamed as six packed joint radians over a local UDP socket at roughly 50 Hz, with per-joint sign and offset correction adjustable from JSON while the simulator keeps running — so calibration is a live loop rather than a restart cycle.

Iteration speed as a design goal

A persistent pick-place loop launches Isaac Sim once and re-reads its tuning parameters every cycle. Grasp depth, object placement and gripper travel are tuned without a relaunch, turning a multi-minute iteration into seconds. Most of the useful parameter search happened only because it became cheap enough to actually do.

LeRobot Studio

A local web app I wrote so teleoperation, recording, training and inference launch without hand-composing CLI invocations. It builds and supervises the commands and reads datasets and checkpoints from disk — it never modifies the LeRobot source tree.
