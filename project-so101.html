Teaching a Robot to Pick and Place Through Demonstration

What does a robot need to see in its demonstrations to learn a physical manipulation task?

I set out to train an ACT policy on a physical SO-101 robotic arm to pick up a cube and place it in a box. I began with randomly collected demonstrations, but the resulting policy failed during autonomous evaluation. Rather than treating this simply as a model failure, I iteratively changed how the demonstrations were collected and used each evaluation to understand what the policy had learned.

Three rounds of data collection took me from complete failure → spatial localization → reliable pick-and-place, revealing how the position and orientation coverage of the demonstrations affected the behavior the policy learned.

The goal

Pick up a cube, place it in a box

I wanted to teach a physical SO-101 robotic arm to pick up a cube and place it in a box using human teleoperated demonstrations.

Attempt 1

Start simple: random object placement

I started with randomly placing the object throughout the workspace and teleoperating the SO-101 to demonstrate the pick-and-place task. I trained an ACT policy on these demonstrations and evaluated it on the physical robot with the cube again placed at random locations.

The result was a complete failure. The robot could not reliably locate, reach or grasp the cube.

Observations

Since I did not record the object position during demonstration collection, it became very tricky to evaluate the performance of the trained ACT policy. I could not figure out why the policy was failing to reach and grasp the object.

Therefore, in the next attempt, I planned to record the cube’s position by dividing the workspace into grids.

Attempt 2

Can spatial structure help?

In order to debug why the ACT policy was not able to learn to pick and place, I decided to make the spatial structure of the demonstrations explicit.

I divided the workspace into six cells and fixed the box in one of them. The cube was then distributed approximately equally across the other five cells during demonstration collection.

I collected the demonstrations, trained ACT again, and evaluated the policy on the physical robot.

Something changed. The policy was no longer completely lost. It learned to localize the cube to the appropriate region and move the arm toward it. However, it still did not reach the precise grasp configuration and could not consistently grasp the cube.

Result

Partial success. The policy learned to move toward the cube’s region but could not reliably grasp it.

Observations

The grid made the policy’s behavior easier to evaluate. It was able to identify the correct grid containing the cube and move toward it, but it could not reliably reach the cube’s exact position and grasp it.

This suggested a more focused next step: keep the cube within a single grid, but densely cover precise positions and multiple orientations. This would allow me to test whether finer position and orientation coverage could enable reliable grasping.

Attempt 3

Structured position + orientation

Instead of spreading the cube across the whole workspace, I confined it to a single cell and marked 19 precise object locations, using a transparent sheet as a positioning template. At each location, I collected demonstrations with four different cube orientations.

The demonstrations now captured not only where the cube was, but also how the robot needed to orient the gripper and approach the cube for each orientation.

The breakthrough

This time, the ACT policy trained on this dataset successfully:

  2. 02Moved toward its position
  3. 03Oriented the gripper
  4. 04Grasped the cube
  5. 05Placed it in the box

Findings

What the experiment taught me

  1. Seen vs. unseen configurations matter. The policy performed reliably when the cube’s position and orientation were represented in the demonstrations, but struggled when evaluated on configurations outside the demonstrated set.
  2. Different orientations require different grasps. I included multiple cube orientations in the demonstrations so the policy could learn the appropriate gripper orientation and approach for each one.
  3. Evaluation requires knowing what the policy has seen. Recording the exact position and orientation of the cube during data collection was essential for evaluating the policy. The policy did not simply learn the abstract task of “pick and place”; its performance depended on the positions and orientations represented in the demonstrations.

Three attempts at a glance

  1. 01Random placementCube placed freely across the workspace.Failed localization + grasp
  3. 02Six spatial regionsBox fixed in one cell, cube across the other five.Localization improved, grasp still failed

Attempt 3 used fewer demonstrations (76, 30,110 frames) than Attempt 2 (101, 55,001 frames), yet produced the more reliable policy within its demonstrated region.

Next question · ongoing

Can a VLA generalize?

Show details

The ACT experiments showed me that the demonstrations strongly influenced which object positions and orientations the policy could handle. I am now investigating whether a pretrained Vision-Language-Action model can learn the same task and generalize beyond the demonstrations.

I have fine-tuned SmolVLA on the six-grid demonstration dataset and am now evaluating it on the physical robot.

  1. Task learning. Can SmolVLA reliably learn the cube pick-and-place task?
  2. Generalization. Can it handle object positions and orientations that were not represented during training?
